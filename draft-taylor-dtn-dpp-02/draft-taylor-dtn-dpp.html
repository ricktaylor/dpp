DTN Peering Protocol

Abstract

This document specifies the DTN Peering Protocol (DPP), an inter-domain routing protocol for the Delay-Tolerant Networking (DTN) ecosystem. DPP facilitates the exchange of reachability information between distinct Administrative Domains (ADs), enabling inter-domain routing across networks operated by different agencies and organizations. DPP separates the control plane from the data plane: a DPP speaker need not be a gateway that forwards bundles, allowing centralized route controllers or orchestration systems to participate in peering on behalf of the gateways they manage.¶

DPP advertises the reachability of bundle nodes in both DTN addressing schemes, ipn (integer-based) and dtn (name-based), and selects routes for both by the same process. DPP speakers authenticate each other using mutual TLS, with each Administrative Domain authorizing its speakers' keys through DNSSEC-signed TLSA records. DPP supports both persistent routes and routes tied to the scheduled contact windows of deep-space networks.¶

1. Introduction

The DTN Peering Protocol (DPP) exchanges reachability information between distinct Administrative Domains (ADs) of a Delay-Tolerant Network. It is an inter-domain routing protocol, analogous in role to BGP [RFC4271] in the Internet, and complements, rather than replaces, the routing that each AD uses internally.¶

A DPP speaker, acting for an AD, establishes an authenticated session with a speaker of a neighboring AD, over which each advertises the bundle nodes that are reachable through its AD, together with the gateway to which bundles for those nodes should be sent and attributes such as when the route is available. A speaker that receives routes selects among them, uses the result to direct the forwarding of bundles by the gateways of its own AD, and may advertise it onward to other ADs, offering transit. How a speaker configures its AD's gateways is outside the scope of this document.¶

1.1. Motivation

Two DPP speakers establish a session to exchange reachability information across administrative boundaries. Common scenarios include:¶

Inter-Agency Connectivity:

Two space agencies operate independent DTN infrastructures. DPP allows their border gateways to advertise which spacecraft and ground stations are reachable through each network, enabling bundles to flow between agencies.¶

Commercial-Government Integration:

A commercial satellite operator peers with a government science network. DPP enables the commercial network to advertise transit capacity to deep-space assets, while the science network advertises its spacecraft endpoints.¶

Redundant Path Discovery:

A Mars relay satellite is reachable via two ground station complexes. If both complexes belong to the same AD, DPP allows both paths to be advertised with appropriate metrics, enabling the network to prefer one path while maintaining the other as backup. If they belong to different ADs, each AD advertises its own path, and peers choose between them by local policy, estimated delivery time, and path length.¶

Scheduled Contact Windows:

Deep-space links are available only during planned contact windows. DPP's time-variant routing attributes allow DPP speakers to advertise future connectivity, enabling proactive bundle scheduling.¶

Independent Intra-Domain Routing:

DPP plays the role of an exterior gateway protocol: it carries routing information between ADs and leaves each AD free to choose how it routes bundles internally. One AD might use Contact Graph Routing (CGR), standardized by CCSDS as Schedule-Aware Bundle Routing (SABR) [CCSDS-SABR]; another might use static routes, or routes computed by its mission planning or network orchestration systems. DPP speakers advertise reachability derived from whatever their AD uses internally, and peers need neither know nor share that mechanism. This also avoids any need for a single contact plan spanning all agencies, spacecraft, and ground stations: each organization maintains its own mission planning systems and contact schedules, and the cost of contact-based routing grows with network size.¶

1.2. Design Principles

Transport:

Messages are defined using Protocol Buffers [PROTOBUF] and carried over gRPC [GRPC], and so over any transport that gRPC supports.¶

Both EID schemes:

Reachability is carried for both the ipn and dtn schemes, and routes for both are selected by the same process.¶

DNS-rooted identity:

ADs are identified by DNS domain names, and each AD uses its own DNS zone to authorize the speakers that act on its behalf.¶

Identity separate from reachability:

An AD's identity is independent of the nodes it advertises routes for.¶

1.3. Scope

This specification defines the protocol messages, state machine, and operational requirements for DPP implementations. It does not define:¶

  • Routing within an Administrative Domain (e.g., CGR or static routing), which is chosen independently by each AD¶

  • How a DPP speaker configures the gateways of its AD with the routes it has selected¶

  • Bundle forwarding algorithms¶

  • Policy languages for route filtering¶

2.1. Definitions

Administrative Domain (AD):

An autonomous unit of routing control identified by a DNS domain name. Examples include agency networks (e.g., dsn.example.org), commercial providers, or research institutions.¶

DPP Speaker:

An entity that participates in the DPP protocol, maintaining peering sessions with other DPP speakers and exchanging route information on behalf of an Administrative Domain. A DPP speaker may be a gateway, a centralized route controller, or any other system authorized to advertise and receive routes for its AD.¶

Gateway:

A bundle node within an Administrative Domain that forwards bundles to or from other ADs. A gateway may or may not also be a DPP speaker. A route names the gateway to which bundles should be sent (Section 7.9).¶

Session:

A connection between two DPP speakers over which routes are exchanged. The speaker that opens the connection is the Initiator; the other is the Responder. Once a session is established the roles are symmetric.¶

Route:

A statement, received over a session, that bundles for the nodes matching an EID pattern can be delivered via the advertising AD, together with the attributes of that statement. A speaker also holds routes that it originates for nodes within its own AD.¶

EID Pattern:

A pattern matching one or more Bundle Protocol [RFC9171] Endpoint Identifiers, as defined in [I-D.ietf-dtn-eid-pattern] and [I-D.taylor-dtn-eid-pattern-dtn].¶

AD_PATH:

The ordered list of Administrative Domains that a route has traversed, most recent first. The last entry is the originating AD. DPP is a path-vector protocol: AD_PATH is used to detect loops and to compare path lengths, as AS_PATH is in BGP.¶

Metric:

A numeric value set by the originating AD to express its preference among routes into its own network. It is analogous to BGP's Multi-Exit Discriminator, with the differences described in Section 8.4.¶

Local preference:

A preference that a speaker assigns to routes by local policy, for example by originating AD or by the peer from which they were learned. It is the first criterion of route selection after specificity (Section 8.5).¶

Transitive:

A flag carried with an attribute whose type the receiving speaker may not understand, telling such a speaker whether to propagate the attribute unchanged when it re-advertises the route, or to drop it (Section 7.5). It says nothing about how a speaker that does understand the attribute treats it.¶

Routing Information Base (RIB):

The set of routes a speaker holds, from all sessions and its own origination, from which it selects routes. This document does not prescribe how it is organized.¶

Forwarding Information Base (FIB):

The table used by a gateway to forward bundles. A speaker populates the FIBs of its AD's gateways from the routes it selects; how it does so is outside the scope of this document.¶

3. Architecture Overview

3.1. Administrative Domains

An Administrative Domain is an autonomous unit of routing control.¶

Identity:

An AD is identified solely by a DNS domain name (e.g., dsn.example.org). Wherever an AD identity appears in DPP, in a Hello, in AD_PATH, or in AD_SET, it is a fully qualified domain name without a trailing dot, with any internationalized labels in A-label form (Section 2.3.2.1 of [RFC5890]). Two AD identities are the same if they are equal under case-insensitive ASCII comparison.¶

Independence:

An AD Identity is distinct from the nodes it advertises. An AD named dsn.example.org may advertise ipn:100.*.* and dtn://rover1.example.org/**.¶

Advertising versus originating:

An AD that advertises a route is not necessarily the AD that originates it. An AD originates routes for the resources it serves, and may also re-advertise routes learned from other ADs, providing transit. The originating AD is identified by the last element of the route's AD_PATH (see Section 7.3).¶

3.2. DPP Speakers and Gateways

DPP speakers and gateways are defined in Section 2.1. A DPP speaker and a gateway MAY be the same node, but they need not be. For example, a centralized route controller may run DPP sessions on behalf of multiple gateways within its AD, naming the appropriate gateway in each route it advertises. This separation of the control plane (DPP) from the data plane (bundle forwarding) allows flexible deployment topologies.¶

An Administrative Domain MAY deploy multiple DPP speakers, each maintaining independent sessions with external ADs. Because loop detection operates on AD identity rather than on individual speaker identity, routes that leave an AD through one speaker and return through another are detected as loops (Section 7.3). Keeping the routes selected by an AD's several speakers consistent with one another, so that the AD's gateways forward coherently, is an intra-domain matter outside the scope of this document.¶

3.3. Deployment Model

DPP depends on DNS, which is not available on spacecraft and requires low-latency connectivity. This is compatible with deep-space deployment because of where the protocol's components sit:¶

DPP speakers are ground infrastructure.

DPP operates at administrative boundaries. DPP speakers are ground stations, mission control centers, network operations facilities, or centralized route controllers, all of which have Internet connectivity and can perform DNS lookups. Gateways, by contrast, are the forwarding nodes that handle bundle traffic and may be located anywhere, including on spacecraft or relay satellites, since they need neither DNS access nor direct participation in DPP.¶

DNS is used at session establishment only.

DPP performs DNS lookups only when establishing a session, to find a peer's DPP service and to retrieve the TLSA records that authorize the peer's key. Once a session is established, no further DNS queries are required, and the session is unaffected by the subsequent unavailability of DNS.¶

Spacecraft do not run DPP.

Spacecraft run the Bundle Protocol and may use CGR or static routing for their own forwarding decisions, under instructions from their ground-based mission operations, which participate in DPP on their behalf. Spacecraft and relay satellites MAY serve as gateways, forwarding bundles between ADs, without participating in DPP: a ground-based DPP speaker advertises routes on their behalf, naming the in-space node as the gateway.¶

As with BGP, the systems that run the routing protocol require connectivity that the systems they route for need not have.¶

4. Trust and Verification Model

DPP uses DNS as the root of trust for Administrative Domain identities. An AD authorizes DPP speakers to act on its behalf by publishing, in its own DNS zone, TLSA records [RFC6698] that identify the speakers' keys. DPP speakers authenticate each other using mutual TLS [RFC8446], and each verifies that the key its peer presented in the TLS handshake is one that the peer's claimed AD has authorized, following the DNS-Based Authentication of Named Entities (DANE) model [RFC7671].¶

Because the key authorized in DNS is the key that authenticates the TLS connection, the proof of authorization is bound to the session itself.¶

In the anticipated mode of operation, a DPP speaker is provisioned with a list of DNS domain names, one for each Administrative Domain with which its operator wishes to peer. The speaker uses each name to verify the identity of the corresponding peer (as described below), and may also use it to discover how to reach that peer's DPP service.¶

4.1. DNS Records at _dpp

An AD publishes the records used by DPP at the owner name _dpp.<AD-Domain>. The _dpp label is specific to this protocol: the records it holds authorize and locate DPP speakers, and do not describe DTN nodes or convergence layers in general.¶

The zone containing these records MUST be signed with DNSSEC [RFC9364].¶

4.1.1. Authorization: TLSA Records

To authorize a DPP speaker to act for an AD Identity (e.g., dsn.example.org), the administrator MUST publish a TLSA record [RFC6698] for the speaker's key at _dpp.<AD-Domain>:¶

_dpp.<AD-Domain>.  IN  TLSA  <usage> <selector> <mtype> <data>
¶

This owner name differs from the _port._protocol.host form described in Section 3 of [RFC6698]: the records authorize keys to act for an AD, not for a service at a particular host and port, so they are published under the AD's own name. A speaker's TLSA record is therefore independent of the speaker's hostname, and of whether the speaker accepts incoming sessions.¶

The consequences of this naming are as follows. The owner name is registered for the TLSA RR type in the "Underscored and Globally Scoped DNS Node Names" registry (Section 11.1), as [RFC8552] requires for any underscored name used with a given RR type. The rules of Section 3 of [RFC6698] and Section 7 of [RFC7671] for deriving the TLSA base domain from a transport name, port, and CNAME chain do not apply: the base domain is always _dpp.<AD-Domain> for the AD named in the peer's Hello, and a speaker MUST NOT derive it from the hostname it connected to or from any SVCB record. The same records serve to authenticate both the Initiator and the Responder, whereas [RFC6698] and [RFC7671] describe the authentication of a TLS server only. Because the certificate usage is DANE-EE(3), the names in the certificate are not checked (Section 5.1 of [RFC7671]), which is what allows the authorization to be independent of the speaker's hostname.¶

The following rules apply:¶

  • The certificate usage MUST be DANE-EE(3) (Section 5.1 of [RFC7671]). Records with any other certificate usage MUST be treated as unusable. As a consequence, a speaker's certificate need not be issued by any certification authority, and the names and validity period in the certificate are ignored; a self-signed certificate is sufficient.¶

  • Implementations MUST support the SubjectPublicKeyInfo selector, SPKI(1), and the SHA2-256(1) matching type. ADs SHOULD publish "3 1 1" records, as recommended in Section 5.1 of [RFC7671]. Because such a record matches the speaker's public key rather than its certificate, it need not change when the certificate is renewed with the same key.¶

Example:¶

_dpp.dsn.example.org.  IN  TLSA  3 1 1 (
    8f3a5e...c2 )
¶

4.1.2. Discovery: SVCB Records

An AD whose DPP speakers accept incoming sessions MAY also publish SVCB records [RFC9460] at _dpp.<AD-Domain>, identifying the hosts and ports at which those speakers can be reached. An Initiator MAY use these records to locate a peer's DPP service, or MAY instead be configured with the peer's address; in the terms of Section 3 of [RFC9460], DPP is SVCB-optional. SVCB records play no part in authorization: a speaker listed in an SVCB record is authorized only if it presents a key matching a TLSA record.¶

4.1.2.1. SVCB Protocol Mapping for DPP

This subsection is the protocol mapping for DPP required by Section 2.4.3 of [RFC9460].¶

Query name:

_dpp.<AD-Domain>, where <AD-Domain> is the AD Identity. Port Prefix Naming (Section 2.3 of [RFC9460]) is not used: the port is carried in the port SvcParam, and no port label is prepended.¶

Authentication name:

None. The TLS connection to a DPP speaker is authenticated against the TLSA records at _dpp.<AD-Domain> (Section 4.1.1), not against the TargetName. The TargetName therefore need not appear in the speaker's certificate, and a speaker reached through an SVCB record is authenticated in exactly the same way as one reached through configuration.¶

Default ALPN set:

h2, since DPP is carried over gRPC [GRPC], which uses HTTP/2. The alpn SvcParam MAY be used to list additional protocols that the speaker supports for gRPC, and no-default-alpn MAY be used to exclude h2. There is no DPP-specific ALPN identifier.¶

Applicable SvcParamKeys:

mandatory, alpn, no-default-alpn, port, ipv4hint, and ipv6hint, with the meanings given in Section 7 of [RFC9460]. Other keys MUST be ignored.¶

Default port:

50051, the port conventionally used by gRPC services. No port is assigned to DPP by IANA; see Section 11.5. A ServiceMode record that lacks the port SvcParam directs the Initiator to the default port (Section 7.2 of [RFC9460]). An Initiator that is configured with a peer's address, rather than discovering it, likewise uses the default port unless configured otherwise.¶

Automatically mandatory keys:

None.¶

AliasMode:

Permitted, but NOT RECOMMENDED, for the reasons given in Section 6 of [RFC9461] for a comparable protocol. Whether or not an alias is followed, the TLSA records used for authorization are those at _dpp.<AD-Domain> for the AD named in the peer's Hello, never those at an alias target.¶

The TargetName of each ServiceMode record SHOULD be the hostname of a DPP speaker. A TargetName of "." would make the _dpp owner name the effective TargetName (Section 2.5.2 of [RFC9460]), requiring address records at a name beginning with an underscore label, which some authoritative servers cannot publish (Section 10.3 of [RFC9460]).¶

Examples, the first using the default port and the second a port chosen by the operator:¶

_dpp.dsn.example.org.  IN  SVCB  1 speaker.dsn.example.org.
_dpp.dsn.example.org.  IN  SVCB  2 backup.dsn.example.org. port=4600
¶

4.1.3. Multiple DPP Speakers per AD

An Administrative Domain that deploys multiple DPP speakers has two options for key management:¶

Shared key:

All speakers share a single key pair. The AD publishes one TLSA record and distributes the private key to each speaker. This is operationally simple but means that compromise of any single speaker exposes the key for the entire AD.¶

Per-speaker keys:

Each speaker has its own key pair, and the AD publishes one TLSA record for each. A peer accepts any speaker whose key matches one of the records.¶

Per-speaker keys provide better operational security: a compromised speaker can be revoked by removing its TLSA record, without affecting other speakers in the AD. The choice between shared and per-speaker keys is a local operational decision and is transparent to the peering partner.¶

The same mechanism allows an AD to delegate peering to a third party, such as a network operations provider that runs DPP speakers on the AD's behalf. The AD publishes a TLSA record for the delegate's key. The delegate never holds a credential for the AD's zone, and the delegation is revoked by removing the record.¶

Example with per-speaker keys, one of them delegated, and SVCB records for the two speakers that accept incoming sessions:¶

; Speakers accepting incoming sessions, on the default port
_dpp.dsn.example.org.  IN  SVCB  1 gds.dsn.example.org.
_dpp.dsn.example.org.  IN  SVCB  2 cbr.dsn.example.org.

; Keys authorized to act for dsn.example.org
_dpp.dsn.example.org.  IN  TLSA  3 1 1 (
    8f3a5e...c2 )   ; Goldstone
_dpp.dsn.example.org.  IN  TLSA  3 1 1 (
    51d07b...9e )   ; Canberra
_dpp.dsn.example.org.  IN  TLSA  3 1 1 (
    a7e214...41 )   ; Delegated operator (initiates only)
¶

4.2. Session Establishment and Authentication

A DPP session is established as follows:¶

  1. TLS: The Initiator opens a TLS connection to the Responder. Both speakers MUST use TLS 1.3 [RFC8446] or later, and both MUST present a certificate: the Responder MUST request a certificate from the Initiator, and MUST abort the connection if none is provided. The Responder does not learn which AD the Initiator claims to represent until it receives the Initiator's Hello, so it accepts the Initiator's certificate provisionally and completes its verification in step 5.¶

  2. Hello: Once the gRPC stream is established, each speaker MUST send a Hello message, containing its local_ad_id, as its first message on the stream. A speaker need not wait for its peer's Hello before sending its own.¶

  3. Peer check: On receiving the peer's Hello, the Initiator MUST reject the session if the local_ad_id it contains is not the AD to which the Initiator intended to connect. The Responder MUST reject the session if the local_ad_id is not an AD with which it is configured to peer. AD identities are compared as described in Section 3.1; a local_ad_id that is not a valid domain name in that form is treated as not matching. Performing this check before the DNS lookup prevents an unauthenticated peer from causing lookups of arbitrary names. Either speaker MUST also reject the session if the version in the peer's Hello is not one it supports, or if the peer's hold_time_seconds is unacceptable (Section 5.2).¶

  4. Lookup: The speaker MUST query DNS for TLSA records at _dpp.<local_ad_id>, where local_ad_id is the AD named in the peer's Hello. The TLSA RRset MUST be validated as secure using DNSSEC. The speaker MUST either perform DNSSEC validation itself, or obtain the validation result from a validating resolver that it trusts and that it reaches over a channel protected from modification, as described in Section 4.9.3 of [RFC4035]; an AD bit received over an unprotected channel MUST NOT be relied on. If the validation state is insecure, indeterminate, or bogus, or the RRset contains no usable records (Section 4.1 of [RFC6698]), the speaker MUST reject the session.¶

  5. Verification: The certificate that the peer presented in the TLS handshake MUST match at least one usable TLSA record, using the DANE-EE matching rules of Section 5.1 of [RFC7671]. If none matches, the speaker MUST reject the session.¶

A speaker that rejects a session SHOULD send a Notification with an appropriate error code before closing the stream. From each speaker's point of view, the session is ESTABLISHED once it has both sent its own Hello and verified its peer's. A speaker MUST NOT send RouteUpdate messages before then.¶

Because the Initiator knows which AD it is connecting to, it MAY perform steps 4 and 5 for the Responder during the TLS handshake itself, and so avoid sending its Hello to an unauthorized peer.¶

DNS is consulted only during session establishment, and an established session is not affected by the subsequent unavailability of DNS. A speaker MAY periodically repeat steps 4 and 5 for an established session, and terminate the session if the peer's key is no longer authorized. A lookup failure during such a check MUST NOT by itself cause the session to be terminated.¶

5. Session State Machine

A conformant DPP speaker MUST implement the following session state machine. The messages it refers to are defined in Section 6. The state machine, like the decision process of Section 8.5, is conceptual: an implementation need not be structured as described, provided that its externally visible behavior is the same.¶

5.1. States

Both speakers run the same state machine. The steps referred to are those of Section 4.2.¶

Table 1
State Description
CONNECTING TLS connection established with certificates exchanged, and gRPC stream open (step 1); the peer is not yet verified. Speaker MUST send its Hello and move to HELLO_SENT.
HELLO_SENT Hello sent; waiting for the peer's Hello. Upon receipt, speaker moves to VERIFYING.
VERIFYING Peer's Hello received. Speaker performs the peer check, DNS lookup, and verification (steps 3-5). On success, it moves to ESTABLISHED.
ESTABLISHED Peer verified. Speaker MAY send and receive RouteUpdate and KeepAlive messages, beginning with the initial route exchange (Section 5.3).
CLOSING The session is ending, because of an error or an orderly close. Speaker MUST send a Notification (if it has not already received one that ends the session, and if it can) and close the stream (Section 5.5).

A Notification MAY be sent and received in any state.¶

A speaker MUST move to CLOSING, sending the Notification code shown, if:¶

  • any step of Section 4.2 fails (UNSUPPORTED_VERSION, UNEXPECTED_AD, AUTHORIZATION_FAILED, or UNACCEPTABLE_HOLD_TIME, as appropriate);¶

  • it receives any message other than Hello or Notification before reaching ESTABLISHED, or a second Hello on the same stream (UNEXPECTED_MESSAGE);¶

  • the session does not reach ESTABLISHED within a locally configured time (UNSPECIFIED);¶

  • the hold timer expires (Section 5.2; HOLD_TIMER_EXPIRED);¶

  • it receives more routes from the peer than it will accept (Section 10.4; MAX_ROUTES_EXCEEDED); or¶

  • it decides to close the session for any other reason (CEASE, or CONNECTION_COLLISION in the case of Section 5.4).¶

A speaker MUST also move to CLOSING, without sending a Notification, if it receives a Notification whose code ends the session (Section 6.7), or if the gRPC stream is closed by the peer or fails.¶

In the ESTABLISHED state, a speaker that receives a PeerMessage whose payload it does not recognize, or a Hello, SHOULD send a Notification with level WARNING and code UNEXPECTED_MESSAGE, and MUST otherwise ignore the message. A speaker MUST NOT send a message defined by an extension that is not in effect on the session (Section 6.3).¶

5.2. Hold Time and Keepalives

Each speaker proposes a hold time in the hold_time_seconds field of its Hello, which MUST be at least 3 seconds. A speaker that receives a Hello with a hold_time_seconds of less than 3 MUST reject the session with code UNACCEPTABLE_HOLD_TIME. The hold time for the session is the smaller of the two values.¶

In the ESTABLISHED state, a DPP speaker MUST send KeepAlive messages at intervals not exceeding one third of the session hold time. If a speaker receives no message from its peer for a period equal to the session hold time, it MUST move to CLOSING with code HOLD_TIMER_EXPIRED.¶

5.3. Initial Route Exchange

On entering ESTABLISHED, a speaker MUST send to its peer every route that it would advertise to that peer at that time (Section 9), followed by a RouteUpdate with initial_update_complete set. That RouteUpdate MAY also carry announcements or withdrawals. A speaker MUST send it even if it has no routes to advertise. The flag MUST NOT be set in any later RouteUpdate on the session, and a receiving speaker MUST ignore it if it is.¶

Until a speaker has received its peer's RouteUpdate with initial_update_complete set, or a locally configured time has elapsed since the session became ESTABLISHED, it SHOULD defer updating its own advertisements to other peers in response to routes received over the new session. Without this, each advertisement received during the initial exchange could change the speaker's selected routes, and so cause a separate update to every other peer. Routes received during the initial exchange MAY be used for forwarding as soon as they are received. This is analogous to the End-of-RIB marker of [RFC4724].¶

5.4. Multiple Sessions and Collisions

A speaker MAY hold sessions with several speakers of the same AD, and an AD's speakers MAY each hold sessions with the same peer; routes received over each session are independent (Section 7.7). Two sessions between the same pair of speakers, which can arise when each is configured to initiate a session to the other, are not harmful, but carry the same routes twice.¶

A speaker identifies a peer speaker by the public key that the peer presented in the TLS handshake (Section 4.2) together with the peer's speaker_id; speakers of one AD that share a key (Section 4.1.3) SHOULD therefore set distinct speaker_id values. When a speaker has two sessions with the same peer speaker, both of which are ESTABLISHED or being established, it SHOULD close one of them with code CONNECTION_COLLISION. So that both speakers close the same session, the session to close is the one initiated by the speaker whose public key has the numerically greater SHA-256 digest, with the digests compared as unsigned big-endian integers. A speaker MUST NOT close a session for this reason until the newer session is ESTABLISHED, so that a session is not lost to a connection attempt that fails.¶

5.5. Session Termination

A speaker that is ending a session, whether because of an error or by choice, MUST send a Notification whose code describes the reason before closing the gRPC stream, if it is able to; CEASE is used for an orderly close with no other cause. It MUST then close the stream. It SHOULD log the reason.¶

A session has ended, from a speaker's point of view, when it has sent or received a Notification whose code ends the session, or when the gRPC stream has been closed by the peer or has failed. When a session ends, the speaker MUST remove all routes received over it (Section 7.7), repeat route selection, and update its advertisements to other peers (Section 9).¶

This document defines no means of preserving routes across a session failure, such as the graceful restart mechanism of BGP [RFC4724], nor of asking a peer to re-send its routes without re-establishing the session. A transient transport failure therefore removes every route learned over the session, including those for contact windows far in the future, until a new session is established and the initial route exchange repeated. Speakers on links prone to interruption SHOULD set hold times accordingly. A mechanism for retaining routes across a brief interruption may be the subject of a future extension.¶

An Initiator whose session has ended MAY establish a new one. It SHOULD wait before doing so, for an interval that increases with each successive failure to establish or keep a session with the same peer, so that a persistently failing peer is not retried continuously; it MAY reconnect promptly after an orderly CEASE.¶

6. Protocol Definition

This section defines the messages exchanged over a session. They are written in the proto3 language of Protocol Buffers and encoded on the wire as specified in [PROTOBUF]; the encoding, not the language, is what an interoperating implementation must match. They are carried over a single bidirectional gRPC [GRPC] stream for the lifetime of the session. The sections that follow specify how the routing messages are processed.¶

A speaker MUST be able to receive a PeerMessage of at least 4 MiB (4,194,304 bytes), the default limit in common gRPC implementations, and MUST NOT send one larger than that. A RouteUpdate whose announcements and withdrawals would exceed the limit MUST be split into several RouteUpdate messages; since each advertisement is complete in itself (Section 7.7), a split has no effect on how the routes are processed, and only the last message of the initial route exchange carries initial_update_complete (Section 5.3).¶

7. Route Attributes and Advertisements

Once a session is established (Section 4.2), DPP speakers share reachability information by sending RouteUpdate messages (Section 6.4), each carrying route advertisements and withdrawals. A route advertisement tells the peer that bundles destined for nodes matching a set of EID patterns can be delivered via the advertising AD, together with the attributes of that route. The form of those patterns, and how a speaker chooses between routes that match the same EID, are described in Section 8; how a speaker derives the routes it advertises from those it has selected is described in Section 9.¶

Routes are identified by the session they were received over and an advertisement ID chosen by the sender (Section 7.7), not by their patterns. This differs from BGP, in which a new advertisement for a prefix implicitly replaces the previous one from the same peer: in DPP, a new advertisement with a different ID adds routes, re-sending an ID replaces them, and a withdrawal names IDs. The model resembles the path identifiers of the BGP ADD-PATH extension [RFC7911].¶

DPP uses an extensible attribute model inspired by BGP path attributes [RFC4271]. This allows new route properties to be defined in future protocol versions without breaking existing implementations.¶

7.1. Advertisement Structure

A RouteAdvertisement (Section 6.4) carries:¶

Advertisement ID:

An id that identifies the advertisement within the session, so that it can later be replaced or withdrawn (Section 7.7).¶

Patterns:

One or more EID patterns identifying the destination nodes, constrained as described in Section 8.2. Each pattern creates a separate route; all share the advertisement's attributes.¶

Attributes:

The properties of the route. Two are always present, ad_path and metric; the rest are optional and are carried in an extensible list.¶

7.2. Required Attributes

Table 3
Field Description
ad_path List of AD domains traversed (for loop detection and path length; see Section 7.3)
metric Originating AD's preference among routes into its network (lower = preferred; see Section 8.4)

7.3. Loop Detection

DPP is a path-vector protocol: routing loops are prevented using the AD_PATH attribute (similar to BGP's AS_PATH [RFC4271]). Loop detection operates on Administrative Domain identity, not on individual DPP speaker identity. This means an AD MAY deploy multiple speakers without risk of routing loops, as all speakers within an AD share the same local_ad_id.¶

Mechanism:

Every route advertisement MUST include the ordered list of ADs it has traversed.¶

Propagation:

When a DPP speaker advertises a route to a peer, it MUST prepend its own local_ad_id to the AD_PATH. This applies both to routes it originates (which therefore carry an AD_PATH containing only the originating AD) and to routes it re-advertises.¶

Verification:

When a DPP speaker receives a route advertisement, the first (left-most) entry in AD_PATH MUST match the local_ad_id that the peer authenticated during session establishment (Section 4.2). If it does not, or if AD_PATH is empty, the advertisement is malformed (Section 7.8). This prevents a peer from omitting its own AD from the path, or from presenting a route as though it had been received directly from another AD.¶

Detection:

If a DPP speaker receives an advertisement whose AD_PATH, or whose AD_SET attribute if present (Section 9.1), contains its own local_ad_id, it MUST NOT create routes from it; the advertisement is treated as a withdrawal of its ID (Section 7.8).¶

AD identities in AD_PATH and AD_SET are compared as described in Section 3.1.¶

valid_until When the advertising AD stops accepting bundles (if omitted: until withdrawn; see Section 7.6)
arrival_time Estimated earliest delivery time at the destination (optional; see Section 7.6)
bandwidth_bps Rate at which the advertising AD accepts bundles, in bits per second
max_bundle_size Largest bundle the advertising AD accepts, in bytes

7.5. Attribute Types and Forward Compatibility

Every route attribute has a type, identified by a number in the "DPP Route Attribute Types" registry (Section 11.4). Each registered type records the protocol version (Section 6.3) that introduced it and the document that defines it. The attributes defined in this document are all introduced in version 1.¶

A RouteAttribute (Section 6.5) carries an attribute in one of two forms: as the typed member of the attribute choice defined for its type, or as an UnknownAttribute, which carries the type number, the attribute's value as an opaque byte string, and a transitive flag that tells a speaker which does not understand the type what to do with it. A sender MUST choose the form as follows:¶

  • An attribute whose type was introduced in a version that the peer supports (that is, not later than the version in the peer's Hello) MUST be sent in its typed form.¶

  • An attribute whose type was introduced in a later version than the peer supports MUST be sent as an UnknownAttribute. Its value is the encoding specified for that type in the document that defines it.¶

  • An attribute whose type is in the Private Use range of the registry MUST always be sent as an UnknownAttribute.¶

The second rule is what makes it possible for a speaker running an older version to carry a transitive attribute defined by a newer one: because Protocol Buffers does not deliver an unrecognized oneof member to the application, a newer typed member would otherwise be silently lost at the first older speaker on the path. The first rule is what allows speakers running the same version to use the typed, more compact and more convenient form between themselves.¶

A receiving speaker MUST handle an UnknownAttribute as follows:¶

  1. If it recognizes the type, it MUST decode the value as specified for that type and process the attribute exactly as if it had been received in typed form.¶

  2. If it does not recognize the type and transitive is true, it MUST preserve the attribute and propagate it, unchanged, as an UnknownAttribute when re-advertising the route.¶

  3. If it does not recognize the type and transitive is false, it MUST discard the attribute and not propagate it.¶

The transitive flag exists only in UnknownAttribute form, and is meaningful only to a speaker that does not understand the attribute's type: it is all that such a speaker can know about the attribute. A speaker that does understand the type MUST handle the attribute as the document defining the type specifies, in particular when re-advertising a route (Section 9), whether the attribute arrived in typed or UnknownAttribute form; and when it sends the attribute in UnknownAttribute form it MUST set the flag as that document specifies. For a Private Use type, the party using it chooses the flag. The flag is deliberately not recorded in the registry: knowing whether an attribute may be passed on unexamined is no substitute for knowing how it is to be processed, and for every attribute defined in this document except ad_set the correct behavior on re-advertisement is to replace or recompute the value, not to propagate it. A document that defines a new attribute type MUST specify its typed RouteAttribute member, the encoding of its value in UnknownAttribute form, the value of its transitive flag, and how a speaker that understands it treats it when a route is re-advertised.¶

7.6. Time-Variant Routing

All times carried in DPP are absolute, and are compared across ADs. DPP speakers MUST therefore keep their clocks synchronized to UTC, for example using NTP. The time windows that DPP describes are typically measured in minutes or hours, so the accuracy that ordinary network time synchronization provides is sufficient; this document defines no tolerance for clock skew, and two times are equal only if they are identical.¶

The valid_from and valid_until attributes allow an AD to advertise reachability that is available only at certain times, as is common in deep-space networks. Together they define the time window during which the advertising AD will accept bundles for the advertised destinations. A receiving AD can use these time windows as input to its own routing, whether that is contact-based (e.g., [CCSDS-SABR]) or not. Routes without these attributes are valid immediately and remain valid until explicitly withdrawn.¶

Because DTN is a store-and-forward network, the window in which an AD accepts bundles need not coincide with the contacts over which it will forward them. An AD with sufficient storage can accept bundles before its next contact towards their destination opens, and hold them until it does; it can also offer a single window that spans several such contacts.¶

A route is available from the time given by its valid_from attribute. A route with no valid_from attribute, or whose valid_from time has passed, is available immediately. A route expires at the time given by its valid_until attribute: once that time has passed, a DPP speaker MUST treat the route as withdrawn. An advertisement that carries both attributes with a valid_until time not later than its valid_from time is malformed (Section 7.8).¶

The optional arrival_time attribute gives the advertising AD's estimate of the earliest time at which a bundle that it accepts at the start of the route's window can be delivered to the advertised destinations. Unlike the time window, which describes only the advertising AD, the arrival time describes the whole path to the destination. It is set by the originating AD, if that AD can estimate when it will deliver bundles, and is updated by each AD that re-advertises the route (Section 9). A route without an arrival_time attribute provides no estimate.¶

An advertisement that carries both an arrival_time and a valid_from, with the arrival_time earlier than the valid_from, is malformed (Section 7.8): a bundle cannot be delivered before it is accepted.¶

Because the availability of routes changes over time, a DPP speaker MUST repeat route selection (Section 8.5) for the current time whenever any of the following occurs, and MUST update its own advertisements if the routes that it would advertise change (Section 9):¶

  • it receives, replaces, withdraws, or loses a route;¶

  • the valid_from time of a route it holds is reached, making the route available;¶

  • the arrival_time of a route it holds is reached, after which the route is estimated to deliver bundles as soon as they are accepted (Section 8.5); or¶

  • the valid_until time of a route it holds is reached, expiring the route.¶

A peer MAY send multiple advertisements for the same pattern, each with its own advertisement ID (Section 7.7), to describe a series of distinct periods in which it accepts bundles, each with its own attributes.¶

7.7. Advertisement IDs and Withdrawal

Each RouteAdvertisement carries an advertisement ID, in its id field, chosen by the sending speaker, that identifies the advertisement within the session over which it is sent. Each EID pattern in the advertisement creates a separate route, which has the advertisement's attributes; the routes created by an advertisement are identified by the session, the advertisement ID, and their patterns. The ID identifies the advertisement as a whole, not any one of the routes it creates.¶

An advertisement ID is meaningful only within the session in which it is used. A speaker MAY choose advertisement IDs in any way, provided that it does not use the same value for two advertisements that it intends to be held at the same time on the same session. When a session ends, all routes received over it are removed, and the advertisement IDs used in it have no significance in any later session.¶

When a DPP speaker receives a route advertisement, it MUST replace all of the routes previously received over the same session with the same advertisement ID, if any, with the routes created by the new advertisement. The replacement is atomic: the new advertisement's patterns, attributes, and time window take effect together, and any pattern that was in the previous advertisement but is not in the new one is removed. This allows a peer to change any aspect of an advertisement, including its time window, by re-sending it with the same advertisement ID, without first withdrawing it.¶

Advertisements with different advertisement IDs are independent, even if they contain the same pattern and their time windows overlap. A speaker can therefore hold several routes for the same pattern from a single peer, for example when a centralized route controller advertises several entry points into its AD, each with a different gateway_eid and metric (Section 7.9). Route selection chooses between them in the same way as between routes from different peers (Section 8.5).¶

A RouteWithdrawal lists, in its advertisement_ids field, the IDs of advertisements to be removed. For each ID that it lists, all routes received over the same session from the advertisement with that ID are removed. If no advertisement with that ID is held, the ID is silently ignored. A peer that wishes to remove only some of the patterns in an advertisement re-sends the advertisement with the same ID, omitting those patterns.¶

A route also ceases to exist when it expires (Section 7.6), and does not need to be withdrawn. When every route created by an advertisement has expired, its advertisement ID is no longer in use.¶

7.8. Malformed Advertisements and Processing Order

A RouteAdvertisement is malformed if any of the following holds:¶

  • it contains no patterns;¶

  • a pattern does not satisfy the constraints of Section 8.2, including the range constraints of Section 8.2.1, or a dtn pattern has an empty authority;¶

  • its AD_PATH is empty, or the first entry of its AD_PATH does not match the AD that the peer authenticated during session establishment (Section 7.3);¶

  • it carries more than one attribute of the same type, whether in typed or UnknownAttribute form, or an UnknownAttribute whose type_id is 0, 65535, or that of a typed member the peer's version supports (Section 7.5);¶

  • it carries both valid_from and valid_until, and valid_until is not later than valid_from (Section 7.6);¶

  • it carries both valid_from and arrival_time, and arrival_time is earlier than valid_from (Section 7.6);¶

  • its gateway_eid is not the administrative endpoint EID of a node, or is a LocalNode EID (Section 7.9); or¶

  • its bandwidth_bps or max_bundle_size is zero; or¶

  • its ad_set is empty, or contains an AD that also appears in its AD_PATH.¶

A pattern whose scheme the receiving speaker does not recognize (that is, an EidPattern in which no known scheme is set) is not malformed: the speaker MUST ignore that pattern and process the remainder of the advertisement. This allows patterns for additional schemes to be defined in future. If ignoring such patterns leaves an advertisement with no patterns, the advertisement is treated as a withdrawal of its ID, without notification.¶

A receiving speaker MUST treat a malformed advertisement as a withdrawal of its advertisement ID: any routes previously received over the session with that ID are removed, and no routes are created. The speaker SHOULD send a Notification with level WARNING and code MALFORMED_UPDATE, identifying the advertisement in the message field, and MUST NOT end the session on account of a malformed advertisement. This follows the approach of [RFC7606] for BGP: ending the session would remove every route received over it, which is a far greater disruption than the loss of the one advertisement, and would make a peer's single error into an outage.¶

An advertisement whose AD_PATH contains the receiving speaker's own AD (Section 7.3) is likewise treated as a withdrawal of its ID, but without a Notification, since receiving one's own routes back is a normal occurrence in a meshed topology.¶

Within a single RouteUpdate, a receiving speaker MUST process the withdrawals before the announcements, and the announcements in the order in which they appear. Consequently, if an advertisement ID appears both in the withdrawals and in an announcement, the announcement takes effect; and if two announcements carry the same ID, the second replaces the first. Senders SHOULD NOT construct such messages.¶

A PeerMessage that cannot be decoded, or that contains a RouteUpdate before the session is ESTABLISHED, is handled as described in Section 5.1.¶

7.9. Gateway EID Resolution

The gateway_eid attribute identifies the gateway node to which bundles matching the advertised patterns should be forwarded. This enables separation of the control plane (DPP session) from the data plane (bundle forwarding).¶

The gateway EID always identifies a gateway in the AD that sent the advertisement, which is the next hop for matching bundles, not a gateway in the originating AD. A speaker that re-advertises a route therefore replaces the gateway EID with one of its own (Section 9).¶

A gateway_eid MUST be the EID of the gateway node's administrative endpoint (Section 4.2.5.2 of [RFC9171]): for the ipn scheme, ipn:A.N.0, the EID with Service Number zero for the node's FQNN (Section 5.7 of [RFC9758]); for the dtn scheme, dtn://<node-name>/, the EID with a zero-length demux (Section 4.2.5.1.1 of [RFC9171]). Any singleton EID of a node may serve as a node ID for it, but the administrative endpoint EID is the one such EID that every speaker will form in the same way, so requiring it ensures that two routes via the same gateway name it identically. An advertisement whose gateway_eid is not of this form, or is a LocalNode EID (Section 5.4 of [RFC9758]), is malformed (Section 7.8). A gateway_eid SHOULD use the same scheme as the route's patterns, since the receiving AD must find a convergence layer path to the gateway using the EID given.¶

Resolution rules:¶

  1. If a gateway_eid attribute is present, forward matching bundles to the node it identifies.¶

  2. If gateway_eid is absent, derive the gateway EID from the peer's AD identity (local_ad_id from Hello) by forming the administrative endpoint EID of a dtn node whose node-name is the AD's domain name:¶

    <dns_name>  ->  dtn://<dns_name>/
    ¶

    For example: dsn.example.org -> dtn://dsn.example.org/¶

    This default assumes that the AD operates a gateway node whose dtn node-name is the AD's own domain name. An AD for which that is not the case MUST include an explicit gateway_eid.¶

No mapping from a domain name to an ipn FQNN is defined: the ipn.arpa zone proposed in [I-D.ek-dtn-ipn-arpa] maps an FQNN to the records needed to reach the node, but does not map a domain name to an FQNN. An AD whose gateways have only ipn EIDs therefore needs to include an explicit gateway_eid attribute.¶

Determining how to reach a gateway at the convergence layer, once its EID is known, is a function of the receiving AD's BP agents rather than of DPP, and can use any available means, including configuration. For a gateway with an ipn EID, [I-D.ek-dtn-ipn-arpa] specifies a DNS-based procedure: for example, TCPCLv4 [RFC9174] endpoints for the node (978879,2) are found from SRV records at _dtn-bundle._tcp.2.978879.ipn.arpa.. No corresponding procedure is currently specified for gateways with dtn EIDs.¶

This allows the following deployments:¶

DNS-named gateway:

An AD with identity dsn.example.org that operates a gateway node named dtn://dsn.example.org/ need not include a gateway_eid. Since no DNS-based procedure is currently specified for discovering the convergence layer endpoints of a dtn node, the receiving AD must be configured with how to reach that gateway; the default saves the attribute, not the configuration.¶

Separated control plane:

A DPP speaker that is not itself a gateway (e.g., a centralized route controller) can advertise routes with explicit gateway_eid pointing to the forwarding nodes within the AD, enabling load distribution or failover.¶

Load distribution:

Multiple routes to the same destination can be advertised with different gateway_eid values and metrics.¶

Examples:¶

# AD identity is DNS name - gateway_eid derived automatically
AD Identity: dsn.example.org
Default Gateway EID: dtn://dsn.example.org/

RouteAdvertisement {
  patterns: [ipn:100.*.*]
  ad_path: ["dsn.example.org"]
  metric: 10
  // No gateway_eid needed - defaults to dtn://dsn.example.org/
}
¶ 
# Explicit gateway_eid for control/data plane separation
AD Identity: dsn.example.org

RouteAdvertisement {
  patterns: [ipn:100.*.*]
  ad_path: ["dsn.example.org"]
  metric: 10
  attributes: [
    gateway_eid: "dtn://ingress1.dsn.example.org/"
  ]
}
¶

8. Addressing and Metrics

This section describes the EID patterns carried in route advertisements (Section 7), and how a DPP speaker selects between routes whose patterns match the same EID.¶

8.1. Routes Identify Nodes

DPP advertises the reachability of bundle nodes, not of the services registered at them. A bundle is forwarded to the node that is the sole member of its destination endpoint; the service or demux component of the destination EID selects an application at that node, after forwarding is complete, and plays no part in it. Routes therefore match nodes:¶

  • For the ipn scheme, a node is identified by its Fully Qualified Node Number (FQNN), the Allocator Identifier and Node Number together. All ipn EIDs that share an FQNN refer to the same node (Section 5.1 of [RFC9758]), so an ipn route pattern specifies Allocator Identifiers and Node Numbers only, and matches every Service Number.¶

  • For the dtn scheme, a node is identified by the node-name in the authority of its EIDs (Section 4.2.5.1.1 of [RFC9171]), so a dtn route pattern specifies an authority only, and matches every demux.¶

In the text form of [I-D.ietf-dtn-eid-pattern], an ipn route pattern always ends in .* (any Service Number), and a dtn route pattern always ends in /** (any demux). The protocol encoding (Section 6.6) omits these components, since they carry no information.¶

Whether a particular service is offered at a node, and whether a gateway will accept bundles for it, are matters of application registration and of local policy at the receiving AD, not of reachability, and are outside the scope of DPP.¶

A node may have EIDs in more than one scheme, for example an ipn EID and a dtn EID. The Bundle Protocol does not define any relationship between such EIDs, and nor does DPP: routes for the two schemes are independent, and a bundle is forwarded using routes for the scheme of its destination EID. Only routes for singleton endpoints are advertised; non-singleton endpoints (Section 4.2.5.1.1 of [RFC9171]) are outside the scope of this document.¶

8.2. EID Pattern Constraints (Monotonic Specificity)

Route patterns are EID Patterns: ipn patterns as defined in [I-D.ietf-dtn-eid-pattern], and dtn patterns as defined in [I-D.taylor-dtn-eid-pattern-dtn], which supplies the scheme-specific dtn pattern that [I-D.ietf-dtn-eid-pattern] leaves to be defined by other documents. To keep specificity comparison and longest-match lookup well-defined, DPP restricts both to a Strict Monotonic Subset.¶

A pattern exhibits monotonic specificity if all wildcard components are confined to the logical "leaves" of the naming hierarchy. A specific child component cannot exist under a wildcard parent. Equivalently, when a pattern is read in hierarchy order, the wildcard (if present) MUST be the terminal element: no specific component, label, or character may follow it. This is what guarantees that any two patterns matching the same EID are nested, keeping both specificity comparison and longest-prefix lookup well-defined.¶

8.2.1. ipn Scheme Constraints (Left-to-Right Hierarchy)

The ipn route pattern hierarchy is Allocator Identifier -> Node Number [RFC9758]; the Service Number is always a wildcard (Section 8.1). If the Allocator Identifier is not specific (i.e., is a wildcard or range), the Node Number MUST be a wildcard.¶

A range MUST consist of a single interval whose size is a power of two, greater than one, and whose lower bound is a multiple of that size (i.e., it is equivalent to a bit prefix, in the same way as an Allocator Identifier range in [RFC9758]). A range of size one MUST be expressed as a specific value. Arbitrary ranges are not permitted, because two overlapping ranges (e.g., [10-13] and [12-15]) can match the same EID without either containing the other.¶

  • Valid: ipn:100.1.* (Specific Node), ipn:100.[12-15].* (Aligned Node Range), ipn:100.*.* (Node Wildcard), ipn:** (Default Route, equivalent to ipn:*.*.*).¶

  • Invalid: ipn:100.1.5 (Specific Service; not a route pattern), ipn:*.1.* (Specific Node under Wildcard Allocator), ipn:100.[10-13].* (Unaligned Range), ipn:100.[0-3,8-11].* (Multiple Intervals).¶

8.2.2. dtn Scheme Constraints

A dtn route pattern is a dtn EID Pattern ([I-D.taylor-dtn-eid-pattern-dtn]) whose demux is ** (Section 8.1) and whose authority is either exact or contains a single wildcard that, in the hierarchy order defined by that document, is the terminal element. Which authority patterns satisfy this condition is determined by [I-D.taylor-dtn-eid-pattern-dtn]; this document imposes only the monotonic requirement above. An advertisement containing a dtn pattern that does not satisfy it is malformed (Section 7.8).¶

For example, dtn://rover1.example.org/** (a single node) and dtn://*.example.org/** (every node whose authority ends in .example.org) are route patterns, and dtn://rover1.example.org/svc is not, since it names a demux.¶

8.3. Specificity Scoring

The specificity score prioritizes Exact Matches over Wildcards, and Longer Prefixes over Shorter Prefixes.¶

  • The specificity score is computed locally by the receiver from the EidPattern. It is NOT transmitted in route advertisements, as it can be deterministically derived from the pattern itself.¶

The score is calculated as a uint32:¶

Score = (IsExact x 256) + LiteralLength
¶
IsExact (0 or 1):

1 if the pattern identifies exactly one node, i.e., its Allocator Identifier and Node Number (ipn) or its authority (dtn) contain no wildcard or range; 0 otherwise.¶

Multiplier (256):

A weight ensuring a pattern for a single node outranks any wildcard pattern matching that node. For ipn patterns the maximum LiteralLength is 64 (2 x 32 bits), which is well below 256. For dtn patterns LiteralLength can exceed 256, but a single-node pattern always has at least as many literal characters as any wildcard pattern that matches the same node, so the ordering is preserved.¶

LiteralLength:

The count of non-wildcard characters (or equivalent bits for integers).¶

8.3.1. dtn Scheme Scoring

For dtn patterns, LiteralLength is the number of characters in the authority, in the text form of [I-D.taylor-dtn-eid-pattern-dtn], excluding wildcard characters. The demux is always ** and is not counted.¶

8.4. The Metric Field

The metric field in route advertisements is analogous to BGP's Multi-Exit Discriminator (MED) [RFC4271]. It allows an Administrative Domain to express a preference for how traffic should enter its network when multiple paths exist.¶

For example:¶

Organization B has two DPP speakers peering with Organization A:
  - Speaker in Europe  (gateway with high latency to Mars relay)
  - Speaker in Australia (gateway with low latency to Mars relay)

Organization B advertises routes to ipn:200.*.* via both speakers:
  - Europe speaker:    metric = 100
  - Australia speaker: metric = 10

Organization A, having learned the same route from both peers,
prefers the lower metric and routes traffic via Australia.

If Organization A instead learns the Australia route only
indirectly, via a third AD, it still prefers Australia over the
direct Europe route: both routes originate in Organization B, and
once Organization B has been chosen as the destination AD, its
metric is applied regardless of AD_PATH length.
¶
Table 7
Property Description
Direction Controls incoming traffic (traffic TO your AD)
Scope Only compared between routes from the SAME origin AD
Preference Lower metric = more preferred
Use Cases Capacity balancing, latency optimization, cost management

The metric is set by the originating AD and propagated unchanged through intermediate ADs: a DPP speaker that re-advertises a route MUST NOT modify its metric. It lets an AD steer incoming traffic without coordination with other ADs.¶

The metric expresses the originating AD's preference between ways into its own network. It does not express a preference between different originating ADs, and is not used to choose between them (see Section 8.5).¶

This is a notable departure from the BGP MED, which is non-transitive: a MED received from a neighboring AS is not propagated to other neighboring ASes, and MEDs are compared only between routes received from the same neighboring AS (Section 5.1.4 of [RFC4271] and Section 9.1.2.2 of [RFC4271]; see also [RFC4451]). The DPP metric is transitive, and is compared between routes with the same originating AD, regardless of the neighbor from which they were learned.¶

8.5. Route Selection Algorithm

When a DPP speaker has multiple routes whose patterns match the same EID, it MUST select the best path in two stages: it first chooses the originating AD (the last entry in AD_PATH) to which bundles for that EID will be sent, and then chooses among the routes to that AD. The process is specified as a sequence of steps for clarity; an implementation MAY compute the result by any means that produces the same outcome. The reasons for the order of the steps are given in Appendix B.¶

Routes that a speaker originates for nodes within its own AD take part in selection alongside routes received from peers. For this purpose an originated route has an AD_PATH containing only the speaker's own AD, the metric and other attributes that the speaker would advertise for it, and is available from when it is configured.¶

A pattern may legitimately be originated by more than one AD. For example, a spacecraft may be reachable through the ground stations of several agencies, each of which originates routes to it. This is expected in DTN, and the selection process is designed for it.¶

Route selection is performed for a time t: the time at which a bundle is, or would be, accepted. A speaker forwarding a bundle that it holds selects a route for the current time; a speaker deciding what to re-advertise selects routes for each time within the windows that it advertises (Section 9). Only routes that have not expired by time t are considered, including routes that are not yet available at time t: in a store-and-forward network, a bundle accepted at time t can be held until such a route becomes available. A route is available at time t if it has no valid_from attribute, or its valid_from time is not later than t. The outcome of selection for the current time changes only at the events listed in Section 7.6, and a speaker needs to repeat selection only when one of them occurs.¶

Stage 1, choosing the originating AD:¶

  1. Specificity: Keep only the routes with the highest Specificity Score (Section 8.3). For example, prefer dtn://rover1.example.org/** over dtn://*.example.org/**.¶

  2. Local preference: If local policy assigns preferences to originating ADs, or to the peers from which routes were learned (for example, to reflect cost, contractual arrangements, or trust), keep only the routes with the highest preference. A speaker with no such policy treats all routes as equally preferred.¶

  3. Earliest delivery: For each route, take its estimated delivery time to be its arrival_time if it has one, and otherwise the time at which it becomes available, given by its valid_from attribute (Section 7.6); in either case, if that time is earlier than t, take it to be t. For each originating AD among the remaining routes, find the earliest estimated delivery time among its routes. Keep only the ADs for which this time is earliest.¶

  4. Shortest path to each origin: For each remaining AD, find the length of the shortest AD_PATH among its routes whose estimated delivery time is the time found in step 3. Keep only the ADs for which this length is smallest. Path length counts entries in AD_PATH only; members of an ad_set attribute (Section 9.1) are not counted.¶

  5. Tie-break between origins: If more than one AD remains, choose the AD with the longest-held route among the routes considered in step 4; if that does not decide, choose the AD whose name sorts first in a case-insensitive comparison. A route's age is the time since the advertisement that created it was first received on its session; replacing an advertisement with the same ID does not reset the age of routes whose pattern it retains.¶

Let T be the earliest time, not earlier than t, at which any of the chosen AD's routes considered in step 4 is available. Stage 2, choosing a route to that AD, from among the routes of the AD chosen in stage 1 that remain after steps 1 and 2 and are available at time T:¶

  1. Lowest metric: Keep only the routes with the lowest metric (Section 8.4), regardless of the length of their AD_PATHs.¶

  2. Earliest arrival: Keep only the routes with the earliest arrival_time. Routes without an arrival_time attribute are considered only if no remaining route has one.¶

  3. Latest expiry: Keep only the routes that remain valid the longest, as given by their valid_until attributes (Section 7.6). A route with no valid_until attribute is treated as remaining valid indefinitely.¶

  4. Shortest AD_PATH: Keep only the routes with the fewest entries in AD_PATH.¶

  5. Oldest route: If more than one route remains, choose the one that has been held the longest, for stability. If more than one route remains, choose the one with the lower advertisement ID, and then the one received over the session whose peer's public key has the numerically smaller SHA-256 digest.¶

Treating a route without an arrival_time as delivering bundles as soon as it accepts them is optimistic, and so favors routes that omit the attribute. For this reason, a speaker that re-advertises a route that carries an arrival_time is required to include one (Section 9), and an originating AD that can estimate when it will deliver bundles SHOULD include an arrival_time in the routes it originates.¶

Selecting a route by this process is meaningful only if each AD along the path forwards bundles according to the route that it advertised. A DPP speaker therefore MUST advertise to its peers, for each pattern and each time, only a route derived from the route that it has selected for that time (Section 9), and MUST NOT advertise a route that it has not selected.¶

A DPP speaker MAY implement additional local policy that further modifies this process, but such policy is outside the scope of this specification.¶

9. Re-advertising Routes

When a DPP speaker re-advertises a route that it learned from a peer, it offers its own AD as a transit path to the route's destinations. Because DTN is a store-and-forward network, most route attributes describe what the advertising AD offers (when it accepts bundles, and which bundles it accepts), rather than properties of the whole path; only AD_PATH, the metric, and the arrival time describe the path beyond the advertising AD.¶

Route selection depends on time (Section 8.5): the route that a speaker selects for a pattern can differ for bundles that it accepts at different times, as routes become available and expire. For each pattern, a speaker that re-advertises routes therefore advertises a series of routes with non-overlapping time windows, such that, for any time t within one of those windows, the advertised route is derived from the route that the speaker selects for time t. If the selected route changes at some time within a window that the speaker would otherwise advertise, the speaker splits that window at that time, and advertises each part with attributes derived from the route selected for it. A speaker MUST NOT advertise a route for a pattern for any time at which it has no route selected for that pattern.¶

Each such window is advertised as a separate advertisement, with its own advertisement ID (Section 7.7), so that the speaker can update or withdraw it independently when the routes it has selected change. The speaker MAY combine several patterns into one advertisement if they share the same time window and attributes.¶

A speaker SHOULD NOT advertise to a peer a route whose AD_PATH, or whose AD_SET (Section 9.1), already contains that peer's AD. The peer would discard such a route as a loop (Section 7.3), so advertising it only consumes capacity and causes needless processing, which matters on intermittent links.¶

When deriving a re-advertised route from a selected route, the speaker MUST update its attributes as follows:¶

AD_PATH:

The speaker MUST prepend its own local_ad_id (Section 7.3).¶

Metric:

The speaker MUST NOT modify the metric (Section 8.4).¶

Gateway EID:

The gateway_eid attribute identifies a gateway in the AD that sent the advertisement, which is the next hop for bundles matching the route (Section 7.9). When the speaker re-advertises the route, its own AD becomes that next hop. The speaker MUST NOT propagate the gateway_eid it received. It MUST either include a gateway_eid giving the administrative endpoint EID of a gateway in its own AD, or omit the attribute, so that the receiving speaker derives the default gateway EID from the re-advertising speaker's AD identity.¶

Time window:

The valid_from and valid_until attributes describe when the re-advertising speaker's own AD will accept bundles for the route's destinations (Section 7.6). The speaker MUST set them to reflect that, and not simply copy the values it received. Because its AD can store bundles until they can be forwarded, the window it advertises MAY begin earlier than the window it received, provided that its AD can hold bundles accepted before the received window opens until it does. The window it advertises MUST NOT end later than the window it received, since bundles accepted after the received route expires cannot be forwarded over it. A single advertised window that spans several received routes for the same pattern arises not by extending any one of them, but from the derivation described above: for each time within the advertised window, the advertised route is derived from the route selected for that time, which may be a route that is not yet available.¶

Arrival time:

If the received advertisement includes an arrival_time attribute, the speaker MUST include an arrival_time giving its own estimate of the earliest time at which a bundle accepted at the start of its advertised window can be delivered to the destination. That estimate MUST NOT be earlier than the received arrival time, since a bundle cannot reach the destination via the next AD sooner than that AD can deliver it. It will typically be later, reflecting the time needed to forward the bundle to the next AD, including any time for which the bundle must be stored. If the received advertisement does not include an arrival_time, the speaker MUST NOT add one.¶

Bundle size:

The max_bundle_size attribute gives the largest bundle that the re-advertising speaker's AD will accept. The speaker MUST set it to reflect that, and not simply copy the value it received. It MAY be larger than the received value if the AD can deliver larger bundles to the next AD, for example by fragmenting them (Section 5.8 of [RFC9171]) or by encapsulating them using Bundle-in-Bundle Encapsulation [I-D.ietf-dtn-bibe] and fragmenting the encapsulating bundle. If the AD can do neither, the advertised value MUST NOT be larger than the received value.¶

Bandwidth:

The bandwidth_bps attribute gives the rate at which the re-advertising speaker's AD accepts bundles for the route's destinations. The speaker MUST set it to reflect that, and not simply copy the value it received. Because the AD can store bundles, this rate is not limited by the rate of later parts of the path, although sustained traffic in excess of those rates will eventually exhaust the AD's storage.¶

AD_SET:

If the selected route carries an ad_set attribute, the speaker MUST propagate it unchanged (Section 9.1).¶

Unknown attributes:

The speaker MUST treat attributes that it does not understand as described in Section 7.5. The definition of any new route attribute MUST specify how the attribute is treated when a route is re-advertised.¶

9.1. Aggregation

A speaker MAY aggregate: that is, in place of the routes it has selected for several patterns, it MAY advertise a single route whose pattern covers them all, so as to reduce the number of routes it advertises. For example, a speaker holding selected routes for ipn:100.[0-3].* and ipn:100.[4-7].* may advertise ipn:100.[0-7].*. An aggregate is derived from a set of selected routes, the component routes, as follows.¶

Pattern:

The aggregate's pattern MUST satisfy Section 8.2 and MUST cover every component route's pattern. A speaker MUST NOT advertise an aggregate whose pattern matches any node for which it has no selected route at any time within the aggregate's window: aggregating MUST NOT claim reachability that the speaker does not have.¶

AD_PATH and AD_SET:

The aggregate's AD_PATH is the longest common prefix of the component routes' AD_PATHs, after the speaker has prepended its own AD to each (Section 7.3); since every component was received from some peer, this prefix always contains at least the speaker's own AD. Every AD that appears in any component's AD_PATH but not in that common prefix MUST be placed in the aggregate's ad_set attribute, together with the members of every component's own ad_set. AD_SET is an unordered set, and is propagated unchanged by speakers that re-advertise the aggregate. Loop detection considers both AD_PATH and AD_SET (Section 7.3). This is the role played by AS_SET in BGP aggregation (Section 9.2.2.2 of [RFC4271]).¶

Metric:

Component routes MUST have the same originating AD and the same metric; routes that differ in either MUST NOT be aggregated together, since the metric is compared only among routes from one originating AD (Section 8.4). The aggregate carries that metric. The originating AD of an aggregate is the last entry of its AD_PATH if the components share one; if the common prefix is shorter than the components' AD_PATHs, the originating AD is not recoverable from the aggregate, and speakers receiving it MUST treat the last AD_PATH entry as the originating AD for the purposes of Section 8.5.¶

Time window and arrival time:

The aggregate's window is set as for any re-advertised route (Section 9), and MUST lie within the intersection of the windows during which the speaker has a selected route for every node the aggregate's pattern matches. If any component carries an arrival_time, the aggregate MUST carry one that is not earlier than the latest of them, since the aggregate claims delivery to every component's nodes.¶

Other attributes:

gateway_eid, max_bundle_size, and bandwidth_bps describe the aggregating speaker's own AD and are set as for any re-advertised route. Component routes whose values for these attributes the speaker would set differently (for example, routes reached through different gateways) SHOULD NOT be aggregated.¶

Because AD_SET loses the order and the per-route composition of the component paths, path length comparison in Section 8.5 counts AD_PATH entries only; an aggregate therefore appears no longer than its shortest component. Operators who prefer precise path information over smaller route tables can disable aggregation.¶

10. Security Considerations

10.1. AD Authentication

DPP authenticates Administrative Domains using mutual TLS and TLSA records published in each AD's DNS zone (Section 4). The security of this mechanism depends on:¶

DNSSEC:

The TLSA records are only as trustworthy as the DNSSEC chain that protects them. For this reason, DPP requires the TLSA RRset to be validated as secure (Section 4.2), and an AD that cannot sign its zone cannot be authenticated. An attacker who can forge a secure DNSSEC response for an AD's zone, for example by compromising the zone's signing keys or those of a parent zone, can impersonate that AD.¶

Private Key Protection:

The private key of each speaker authorized by a TLSA record MUST be protected. Compromise of a key allows impersonation of the Administrative Domain until its TLSA record is removed and the record's DNS TTL and signature validity have expired. Speakers that periodically re-verify their peers' authorization (see Section 4.2) limit the lifetime of an established session using a revoked key.¶

Channel binding:

Because the key authorized in DNS is the key that authenticates the TLS connection, an attacker cannot relay one speaker's authentication to a different session. An application-layer proof of key possession, such as a signed nonce, would not have this property unless it were explicitly bound to the TLS session.¶

DNS lookups triggered by unauthenticated peers:

The Responder looks up TLSA records for an AD named by a peer whose authorization has not yet been verified. Restricting lookups to ADs with which the Responder is configured to peer (Section 4.2) prevents a peer from causing lookups of arbitrary names.¶

Misdirected SVCB records:

An SVCB record at _dpp.<AD-Domain> can direct an Initiator to any host and port. Because the resulting TLS connection must be authenticated against the AD's TLSA records before any DPP message is acted on, a forged or misconfigured SVCB record can cause connection attempts to a host of the attacker's choosing, but cannot produce an authenticated session with it. Initiators SHOULD apply the same restrictions on destination ports to SVCB-derived addresses as to configured ones (Section 12 of [RFC9460]).¶

10.2. Transport Security

All DPP sessions use TLS 1.3 [RFC8446] or later, with both speakers authenticated, as specified in Section 4.2. This protects the confidentiality and integrity of route advertisements in transit, and ensures that every message on a session comes from the authenticated peer.¶

10.3. Route Injection Attacks

A malicious or compromised DPP speaker could advertise routes for destinations it does not legitimately serve. Implementations SHOULD support policy-based route filtering, so that operators can restrict the patterns accepted from each peer, and SHOULD provide the means to monitor route changes. Operators are advised to confirm, through channels outside DPP, that a peer is entitled to advertise routes to destinations that matter to them.¶

Specificity is the first step of route selection, ahead of local preference (Section 8.5). A malicious AD can therefore attract traffic for part of another AD's destinations by advertising a more specific pattern than the legitimate originating AD does: for example, ipn:100.5.* within another AD's ipn:100.*.*, or dtn://rover1.example.org/** within another AD's dtn://*.example.org/**. Such a route is preferred for the EIDs that it matches, whatever its local preference, delivery time, or path length, so none of the later selection steps limits its effect. This is the DPP equivalent of a BGP sub-prefix hijack. Until origin authorization is available (see below), the principal defense is route filtering: operators SHOULD configure, for each peer, the patterns that they are prepared to accept from that peer, and SHOULD reject more specific patterns than they expect.¶

The AD_PATH verification in Section 7.3 ensures that the first entry in AD_PATH is the authenticated peer, but a receiving speaker cannot verify the remaining entries: a malicious peer can still add or remove ADs further along the path, including the originating AD. Aggregation (Section 9.1) widens this: because an aggregate's AD_PATH is only the common prefix of its components' paths, a transit AD can shorten the apparent path of a route by aggregating it, legitimately or otherwise, and so make it more attractive in selection. Operators who do not trust a peer to aggregate honestly can apply local preference to routes that carry an ad_set.¶

Similarly, the metric is not protected. A malicious transit AD could lower the metric of routes it re-advertises, in violation of Section 8.4, and so attract traffic for the originating AD, even over a longer path: once that AD has been chosen as the destination, the metric takes precedence over AD_PATH length (Section 8.5). Because metrics are not used to choose between originating ADs, such manipulation cannot divert traffic from one originating AD to another, but it can divert traffic between routes into the same AD. Operators that are concerned by this can apply local preference to the peers from which they learn routes, or ignore the metric on routes from untrusted peers.¶

The time window, arrival time, bundle size, and bandwidth attributes are not protected either. Because estimated delivery time is used to choose between originating ADs (Section 8.5), a malicious transit AD that advertises an arrival_time earlier than the one it received, in violation of Section 9, or that omits the arrival_time it received, can attract traffic away from other originating ADs, not just between routes into the same AD. Local preference, which is applied before estimated delivery time, can be used to limit the influence of untrusted peers.¶

The time window, bundle size, and bandwidth attributes describe what the advertising AD itself offers, and a transit AD can legitimately advertise values that differ from those it received, in either direction (Section 9). A receiving speaker therefore cannot detect a false value by comparing it with the values advertised by other ADs on the path. An AD that overstates what it can store or forward can attract bundles that it then fails to deliver; the consequences are similar to those of any other AD that accepts bundles and discards them.¶

Mechanisms that could address these threats, analogous to those used to secure BGP, are discussed in Appendix C.¶

10.4. Denial of Service

DPP speakers are potentially vulnerable to denial of service attacks:¶

Connection exhaustion:

Implementations SHOULD limit the number of concurrent sessions and implement rate limiting for new connections.¶

Route table exhaustion:

Implementations SHOULD limit the number of routes accepted from a single peer, and close the session with code MAX_ROUTES_EXCEEDED when the limit is exceeded (Section 5.1).¶

Keepalive flooding:

Implementations SHOULD rate-limit incoming messages.¶

10.5. Privacy Considerations

Route advertisements reveal network topology and reachability information. Operators should consider whether this information is sensitive before establishing peering relationships.¶

Appendix A. Relationship to DNS-Based Convergence Layer Discovery

This approach aligns with [I-D.ek-dtn-ipn-arpa], which uses DNS for convergence layer endpoint discovery (see also Section 7.9). The two use separate parts of the DNS namespace because they name different things. Names beneath ipn.arpa identify BP nodes, the destinations and next hops of bundles, and are administered by the Allocators that assign their Node Numbers. DPP speakers are not necessarily BP nodes, and act for ADs rather than for Allocators: an AD may hold several Allocator Identifiers, or none. DPP speakers are therefore identified, authorized, and located under the AD's own domain name, at _dpp.<AD-Domain>, and not beneath ipn.arpa. Because the _dpp label is specific to DPP, it does not conflict with the names used for convergence layer discovery (e.g., _dtn-bundle._tcp), or with those that future specifications may define for other DTN services.¶

DNS discovery under ipn.arpa and DPP are complementary sources of forwarding information. An Allocator can publish, for each of its nodes, the convergence layer endpoints of an ingress service that accepts bundles for that node, as in the examples of [I-D.ek-dtn-ipn-arpa], giving other networks a simple, static means of reaching the node without peering. DPP provides what such discovery does not: reachability that varies with time, transit through intermediate ADs, and selection between several ADs through which the same destination can be reached. How a BP agent combines forwarding information from the two sources is a matter of local forwarding policy, as [I-D.ek-dtn-ipn-arpa] also notes.¶

Appendix B. Design Rationale for Route Selection

This appendix explains the choices made in Section 8.5. It is not normative.¶

Estimated delivery time is compared before path length because, in a DTN, when a bundle can be delivered typically matters far more than the number of ADs that it traverses. Arrival time is used where it is known, because in a store-and-forward network the time at which an AD will accept a bundle can be much earlier than the time at which the bundle will be delivered. Where no arrival time is known, the time at which a route becomes available is used instead; for routes that are already available, step 3 then has no effect, and the choice between ADs is made on path length.¶

Stage 2 considers only those routes to the chosen AD that are available at the time at which the route for which it was chosen becomes available. The originating AD's metric therefore chooses between entry points into that AD that are available at that time, even if the entry point it prefers has a later arrival time; as with path length, this is the originating AD's decision. The metric cannot delay traffic to wait for a preferred entry point that becomes available later. Among routes of equal metric, the one that delivers bundles soonest is preferred, and then the one that remains valid the longest, reducing both the risk that a route expires before bundles are forwarded and the frequency with which the selection changes.¶

Metrics are not consulted in stage 1, and are compared only between routes with the same originating AD. Choosing the originating AD before considering metrics means that the outcome does not depend on the order in which routes are compared. It also means that a new route originated by an AD can only make that AD more likely to be chosen (if its estimated delivery time is earlier, or the same over a shorter AD_PATH), or change the route by which that AD is reached; it can never cause some other AD to be chosen instead.¶

As a result, the selected route is not necessarily the shortest or the fastest route available. The originating AD's metric can direct traffic over a longer or slower path into that AD, and the AD so chosen can be reached over a longer path than some route to a different AD. This is intended: it allows the originating AD to control how traffic enters its network, while the choice between ADs is made on local policy, estimated delivery time, and path length alone.¶

This process differs from the BGP decision process (Section 9.1.2.2 of [RFC4271]), which compares AS_PATH length before the MED, and compares MEDs only between routes received from the same neighboring AS. Because that comparison is not a total order, the BGP decision can depend on the order in which routes are compared, which can cause persistent route oscillation [RFC3345]. Choosing the originating AD first, without reference to metrics, avoids this.¶

Appendix C. Future Route Security Mechanisms

This appendix outlines mechanisms, analogous to those used to secure BGP, that could address the route injection threats described in Section 10.3. None is specified here; they are recorded to inform future work. BGP relies on the Resource Public Key Infrastructure (RPKI) [RFC6480], a certificate hierarchy that follows the allocation of IP addresses and AS numbers; DPP uses neither, and DNS, signed with DNSSEC, already provides a suitable hierarchy, as it does for the authorization of DPP speakers (Section 4.1.1).¶

Origin validation:

Comparable to BGP Route Origin Validation [RFC6811], the holder of a namespace could publish in DNS which ADs are entitled to originate patterns within it, and a receiving speaker could check the originating AD in AD_PATH against that list, which would also address the sub-pattern hijack described above.¶

For ipn patterns, the allocation model of [RFC9758] provides the necessary hierarchy. IANA assigns Allocator Identifiers, and each Allocator assigns Node Numbers within its own space, much as IP address space is delegated from IANA to Regional Internet Registries and then to network operators. The ipn.arpa zone proposed in [I-D.ek-dtn-ipn-arpa] mirrors this hierarchy in DNS: a zone for each Allocator Identifier, at A.ipn.arpa., would be delegated to the nameservers of the Allocator that holds it, and can be linked into the DNSSEC chain of trust of ipn.arpa. An Allocator could publish in its zone the ADs entitled to originate routes for its Node Numbers, much as a Route Origin Authorization in the RPKI does for a range of IP addresses; such authorizations would need to be signed, so the Allocator's zone would need to be signed. Because ipn.arpa represents each Node Number as a single decimal label, ranges of Node Numbers do not correspond to DNS subtrees, so authorizations for ranges would be published as data at the Allocator's name rather than beneath it. The range constraints of Section 8.2, under which every range corresponds to a bit prefix, would allow these authorizations to be expressed compactly and aligned with the patterns that DPP carries. The record formats would need to be defined.¶

[I-D.ek-dtn-ipn-arpa] excludes the Default Allocator (Allocator Identifier zero) and the Experimental Use Allocator Identifiers from publication in the global DNS. Origin validation based on ipn.arpa would therefore not be available for patterns within those Allocators' spaces, which include the Node Numbers that the Default Allocator has assigned to existing deployments. Other means, such as route filtering, would continue to be needed for them.¶

For dtn patterns, the holder of the DNS name in the pattern's authority could publish this information under that name.¶

Unlike route origin validation in BGP, more than one AD would commonly be authorized for a pattern, since a destination may legitimately be reachable through several ADs. As with the guidance on the use of maxLength in the RPKI [RFC9319], authorizations would need to cover only the patterns that are actually advertised: an authorization that also covered unadvertised more specific patterns would allow a malicious AD to advertise them with a forged originating AD in its AD_PATH, a sub-pattern hijack that neither origin validation nor competing legitimate routes would prevent.¶

Neighbor authorization:

Comparable to Autonomous System Provider Authorization [I-D.ietf-sidrops-aspa-verification], each AD could publish, alongside its TLSA records, the ADs that it authorizes to re-advertise its routes. A receiving speaker could then check that each pair of adjacent ADs in AD_PATH is so authorized. This would detect most forged paths, including a malicious AD that appends a legitimate originating AD to its own path, without requiring routes to be signed.¶

Path signatures:

Comparable to BGPsec [RFC8205], each speaker could sign each route that it advertises, using the key authorized by its AD's TLSA records, so that a receiving speaker could verify every entry in AD_PATH. Unlike BGPsec, such signatures could also cover the metric, which is not modified in transit, and the arrival time advertised at each hop, so that a receiving speaker could check that no transit AD advanced it.¶

Any of these mechanisms would require DPP speakers to look up DNS records while processing routes, rather than only during session establishment (Section 4.2). Their design would therefore need to consider the volume of such lookups, the caching of their results, and how a failure to obtain or validate DNS records is treated. In particular, an attacker able to block DNSSEC validation of the records for a single pattern must not thereby be able to cause a route that would otherwise be rejected to be accepted.¶
